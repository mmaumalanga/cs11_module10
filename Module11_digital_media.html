1. Digital Publications & Graphics Basics

Digital publications — ebooks, online newspapers and magazines, blogs — move traditional print media onto screens. An ebook is a book in digital format read on an ereader, smartphone, tablet, or PC. In digital graphics, a pixel (short for "picture element") is one small point in the grid that makes up a digital image. Bitmap (raster) graphics store the color of every pixel — great for photorealistic detail, but they pixelate when enlarged. Vector graphics instead store geometric descriptions of shapes, so they stay crisp at any size and are ideal for logos and clip art, though impractical for photo-quality images.

⚠ Common Misconception #1

"Bitmap and vector images are basically interchangeable — you can just pick whichever."
Not really. A bitmap image records the exact color of every pixel, which is why it can capture photo-realistic detail but blurs or "pixelates" when stretched larger than its original size. A vector image stores mathematical shape descriptions instead of pixel colors, so it scales to any size with no loss of quality — but it can't practically represent a photograph. The right choice depends entirely on what you're making: photos need bitmap; logos and clip art need vector.

🌍 Real-World Application

Amazon's Kindle, released in 2007, popularized ebooks by pairing a lightweight, paperback-sized device with epaper — a non-backlit display many readers find easier on the eyes — and a wireless library of purchasable titles. It's a direct example of a digital publication technology succeeding because of good design choices, not just digitization for its own sake.

2. Color, 3D & Animation

RGB color is the additive color model used by screens: red, green, and blue light combine to create every color a pixel displays. Hexadecimal color uses base-16 digits (0–9, then A–F) to write RGB values compactly, like #00FF00 for pure green. CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Black) is the subtractive model used for print, describing color from light reflected off ink or paint rather than light itself. 3D modeling (or ray tracing) builds rotatable 3D objects; rendering calculates how light interacts with a scene and produces the final 2D image. Animation displays a rapid sequence of frames (typically 24–30 per second) to create the illusion of motion.

📐 Worked Example — Counting RGB Colors

Problem: Standard RGB color uses one byte (8 bits) each for red, green, and blue — 24 bits total. How many distinct colors can this represent?

  1. One byte per channel: 8 bits = 28 = 256 possible intensity values for red alone (0–255).
  2. Same for green and blue: Each channel independently offers 256 values.
  3. Combine all three: 256 × 256 × 256 = 224.
  4. Answer: 16,777,216 colors (just over 16 million) — exactly what the reading means by "more than 16 million total possible colors."

✏️ Try It Yourself

A simple monochrome (grayscale) image uses just one byte (8 bits) total, rather than three. How many distinct shades of gray can it represent? (Use the same doubling logic: 2bits.)

⚠ Common Misconception #2

"RGB and CMYK are just two names for the same color-mixing method."
They work in opposite ways. RGB is additive — it mixes colored light, which is why combining red and green light produces yellow on a screen. CMYK is subtractive — it mixes ink or pigment, which is why (as any kindergartner blending paints knows) yellow and red combine to make orange, not the same result as mixing light. A color that looks right on your screen (RGB) can print differently (CMYK) for exactly this reason.

3. Digital Audio & Video

Digital audio stores sound as 1s and 0s through a process called sampling — measuring a sound wave's amplitude at regular intervals (the sampling rate). A higher sampling rate captures the wave more frequently and reproduces it more accurately. ADC (analog-to-digital conversion) turns a real sound wave into digital samples; DAC (digital-to-analog conversion) turns it back into sound through a speaker. Digital video is a rapid sequence of frames (24–30 fps), and video compression keeps file sizes manageable by storing only the pixels that change between frames.

📐 Worked Example — Estimating Raw Video Storage

Problem: Raw, uncompressed digital video requires roughly 1 GB of storage per hour. About how much storage would 3.5 hours of raw footage require?

  1. Identify the rate: 1 GB per hour of raw video.
  2. Multiply by the duration: 1 GB/hour × 3.5 hours.
  3. Answer: 3.5 GB — which is also why compressing video before long-term storage (down to well under 10 GB per movie for a DVD) matters so much.

✏️ Try It Yourself

Using that same rate (roughly 1 GB per hour of raw video), about how many GB would 6 hours of raw, uncompressed footage require?

🌍 Real-World Application

Digitized sound isn't just for entertainment. Forensic audio analysts use digital sound processing to remove noise and identify recordings — a technique called voice-print identification has been used as evidence in thousands of criminal cases. Marine researchers at the Australian Marine Mammal Research Centre use similar digital recording techniques to study the songs of migrating whales.

⚠ Common Misconception #3

"A higher sampling rate mainly makes a recording louder."
Not quite. The sampling rate controls how often per second a sound wave's amplitude is measured — its temporal accuracy — not its volume. A CD samples audio 44,100 times per second, while a phone call samples only about 8,000 times per second; the CD isn't louder, it's a more accurate reproduction of the original wave. Volume is a completely separate property of the signal itself.

4. Digital Interactive Media: Games, VR & AR

Video game consoles pair a fast processor with a graphics coprocessor for real-time 3D rendering; some, like the Wii, use a motion-sensitive controller for social, group-friendly gameplay. Virtual reality (VR) is a computer-simulated 3D environment a user can explore; immersive virtual reality fully surrounds the user in that world through a headset, blocking out the real world. Augmented reality (AR) instead overlays computer-generated images or information on top of the real world you can still see — through a phone camera or smart glasses.

🌍 Real-World Scenario — Analyze

Nintendo's Pokémon GO let players wander around town, pointing their phone's camera at real streets and parks to see Pokémon characters appear as if they were really there. It reached 50.2 million monthly active users shortly after launch.

Which technology powers this core experience?

⚠ Common Misconception #4 (Bonus)

"Virtual reality and augmented reality are basically the same technology."
They're opposites in one key way. Immersive VR replaces your view entirely with a computer-generated world — you can't see the real room around you. AR does the reverse: it keeps the real world fully visible and adds digital content on top of it, like Pokémon GO characters on a real sidewalk or a Google Glass notification at the corner of your eye. One replaces reality; the other augments it.

1. Choosing the Right Graphics Format

A local bakery wants one logo they can use everywhere: on business cards, on their website, and blown up huge on a storefront sign.

Apply what you learned about bitmap vs. vector graphics to their situation: which file type would you recommend for the logo, and specifically what would go wrong if they used the other type at storefront-sign size?

Vector is the right call here. A vector logo stores geometric shape descriptions rather than pixel colors, so it can be scaled from a business card up to a storefront sign with zero loss of quality. If the bakery instead saved the logo as a bitmap (JPG/PNG) at business-card size and then stretched it to sign size, every pixel would be stretched along with it, producing the blurry, blocky "pixelation" the reading warns about — the exact failure mode bitmap graphics have when enlarged beyond their captured size.

2. Sampling Rate vs. Frame Rate

Digital audio (sampling rate) and digital video (frame rate plus compression) both work by breaking a continuous, real-world experience into discrete captured moments per second.

Analyze the similarities and differences between how sampling rate affects audio quality and how frame rate/compression affects video quality. What do the two techniques have in common, and where do they diverge?

In common: both are a "more frequent measurement = more accurate reproduction" trade-off — a CD's 44,100 samples/sec captures a sound wave far more faithfully than a phone call's 8,000, the same way more frames per second captures motion more smoothly. Where they diverge: sampling rate is almost purely about accuracy of reproduction, while video adds a second layer — compression — that deliberately throws away redundant information (unchanged pixels between frames) specifically to save storage, something audio sampling doesn't do in the same way. Video is optimized for both temporal accuracy *and* file size at once; audio sampling rate is mostly just about accuracy.

3. Was AR the Right Choice for Pokémon GO?

Pokémon GO reached 50.2 million monthly active users through augmented reality, letting players see game characters overlaid on real streets and parks through their phone camera — then lost roughly half of those users within a few months.

Evaluate whether augmented reality was the right technology choice for a game meant to hold players' attention long-term. What criteria would you use to judge that, and what, if anything, would you have done differently?

There's no single "correct" verdict here — that's what makes it an evaluation, not a recall question. But a strong evaluation names real criteria (initial adoption/reach, retention over time, cost of upkeep, novelty vs. sustained gameplay depth) before making a judgment. On reach, AR was clearly the right call — the real-world overlay was the entire novelty that drove 50 million users in weeks. On retention, the reading's own numbers (losing about half those users within months) suggest AR got people in the door but wasn't, by itself, enough to keep them — which points toward needing deeper game mechanics layered on top of the AR novelty, not necessarily a different core technology.
